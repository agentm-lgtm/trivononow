Our Story

Trivono Now began with a single rolling pin and a borrowed stove.

Trivono Now is a small pasta shop that treats the daily dough as the heart of everything we do. Our kitchen starts before sunrise, mixing flour and eggs in a wide wooden trough, kneading until the mass turns smooth and elastic, then letting it rest while the ovens warm. We believe a bowl of pasta is a quiet kind of comfort, the sort that asks nothing more than a hungry table and a little patience.

Our shelves hold shapes cut by hand and by bronze die, sauces that have simmered for the better part of a morning, and ready trays built for carrying home. We source grain from regional mills, tomatoes from growers who pick at peak ripeness, and herbs from a small garden bed behind the shop. Nothing here is rushed, and nothing leaves the counter that we would not eat ourselves.

The name Trivono Now was chosen to capture the feeling of a corner kitchen — a place where the food is ready now, warm now, waiting now. We are not chasing trends or grand titles. We are simply rolling, cutting, and cooking, day after day, for neighbors who keep coming back for the same familiar bowl.

Our History

A timeline of flour, fire, and a growing neighborhood table.

2009 — The First Rolling Pin

A borrowed stove and a single recipe

The shop opened in a narrow storefront with one counter, one stove, and a recipe for tagliatelle handed down through three generations. The first morning produced enough noodles for a dozen bowls, and they sold out before noon.

2012 — The Bronze Die

Adding short cuts and ridged shapes

A second-hand bronze extruder arrived in a borrowed van, letting us add fusilli, penne, and rigatoni to the daily rotation. The rough surface of bronze-cut pasta changed how our sauces clung, and regulars noticed the difference immediately.

2015 — The Garden Bed

Herbs grown behind the shop

We turned a strip of cracked pavement behind the kitchen into a raised herb bed. Basil, sage, rosemary, and oregano now travel only a few steps from soil to saucepan, picked the same morning they are cooked.

2018 — The Ready Tray Counter

Baked meals to carry home

Neighbors kept asking for something they could take home already warm, so we built a small bake counter. The first golden tray of baked penne sold out in an afternoon, and the ready meals became a staple of the shop.

2021 — The Wheat Project

Partnering with regional mills

We began working with two regional stone mills to source grain grown within a short distance of the shop. Fresh-milled flour brought a deeper color and a livelier flavor to the dough, and we have never gone back.

Today — Trivono Now

The same rolling pin, a fuller table

The kitchen still starts before sunrise, the dough still rests overnight, and the rolling pin from the first morning still hangs above the counter. What began as a dozen bowls now fills a neighborhood table, but the hands and the patience have not changed.

The Craft Behind Every Strand

A closer look at how dough becomes dinner.

1. The Mix

Flour is mounded on a wooden board, a well is pressed into the center, and eggs are cracked inside. We fold the flour in slowly with a fork until a shaggy dough forms, then bring it together by hand.

2. The Knead

The dough is pushed, folded, and turned for a full ten minutes until it turns smooth and springs back at the touch. This is where the structure is built, strand by strand.

3. The Rest

Wrapped and set aside, the dough relaxes so the gluten softens. A rested dough rolls thin without springing back, the secret to a tender final bite.

4. The Roll

Passed through graduated rollers, the dough thins to a translucent sheet. We dust with semolina between passes so nothing sticks and nothing tears.

5. The Cut

Sheets are trimmed and cut by hand or bronze die into the day’s shapes. Each cut is checked for width and edge, then dusted again to keep the pieces separate.

6. The Cook

Dropped into well-salted rolling water for a brief, bright minute. Fresh pasta cooks in a fraction of the time, and we drain it while it still has a gentle heart.

Wheat & Grain Notes

Where the flour comes from and why it matters.

Stone-Milled Soft Wheat

Our egg dough begins with stone-milled soft wheat from a regional mill that grinds slowly and cool, keeping the grain’s natural oils intact. The flour arrives fresh each week, never sitting in a warehouse, and you can taste the difference in the color of the dough — a warm gold rather than a flat white.

Durum Semolina for Short Cuts

For our short, bronze-cut shapes we use durum semolina, a harder grain that holds its shape in the water and gives a firm, satisfying bite. The semolina is sifted to a consistent granule so every batch extrudes evenly through the dies.

Pasta Guides & Kitchen Notes

Small lessons we have learned at the counter.

How to Cook Fresh Pasta

Fresh pasta is not dried pasta and should not be treated the same. Bring a generous pot of water to a rolling boil, salt it until it tastes faintly of the sea, and drop the pasta in. Fresh strands need only a minute or two — watch for the moment they float and brighten in color. Drain at once and toss with warm sauce so the strands drink it in.

  • Use more water than you think you need.
  • Salt the water, not the dough.
  • Never oil the water — it stops sauce from clinging.
  • Save a splash of cooking water to loosen the sauce.

Storing Fresh Pasta

If you are not cooking it the same day, dust the pasta generously with semolina and lay it flat, not piled, so the pieces do not clump. Kept cool and covered, fresh pasta holds for a day or two. For longer keeping, freeze it spread on a tray first, then bundle it once firm so the strands do not freeze into a single block.

  • Dust with semolina, not flour, to prevent sticking.
  • Keep shapes separated, never heaped.
  • Freeze flat before bundling together.
  • Cook from frozen — do not thaw first.

Reading a Good Sauce

A sauce is ready when it coats the back of a spoon and holds a line drawn through it. If it pools and runs, it needs more time. If it seizes and tightens, a spoonful of cooking water will bring it back to a glossy flow. Taste at the end, not the beginning, because salt concentrates as the water leaves.

Choosing Your Shape

Let the sauce choose the shape. Smooth, thin sauces love long strands. Chunky, robust sauces prefer short cuts with ridges and hollows. Cream sauces wrap gently around ribbons, while pesto tucks into coils. When in doubt, ask the counter — we pair sauce and shape every morning.

Frequently Asked Questions

A few answers from the counter.

Is the pasta made fresh every day?

Yes. The dough is mixed and cut each morning before the shop opens, and we make only as much as we expect to sell that day so nothing sits around.

Do you offer vegetarian options?

Many of our shapes and sauces are vegetarian, including the basil pesto, garlic and herb cream, and all of the ravioli fillings except where noted. Ask the counter for the current vegetarian list.

Can I order a tray for a gathering?

We prepare larger baked trays for gatherings with a day’s notice. Tell us how many people you are feeding and we will suggest the right size and shape.

Where does your flour come from?

Our soft wheat and durum semolina come from two regional stone mills that grind slowly and deliver fresh each week. We also use a single-field farro for limited heritage batches.

How should I store pasta I take home?

Dust it with semolina, keep it flat and covered, and cook it within a day or two. For longer keeping, freeze it flat first, then bundle, and cook straight from frozen.

Do you have a seasonal menu?

Yes. Our ready trays and several sauces rotate with the seasons, following what is at its best in the garden and from our growers.
